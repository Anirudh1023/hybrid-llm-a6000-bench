Statement of Purpose
Anirudh Bocha · Master of Science in Intelligent Information Systems (MIIS), Carnegie Mellon University
Deploying speech, language, and multimodal models in real-world settings exposes a recurring gap between what pretrained foundation models compute by default and what a downstream task or deployment environment can actually support. I first saw this in my undergraduate speech research at IIIT Hyderabad, where an early intermediate layer of HuBERT outperformed its final layer at detecting clinical speech disfluencies while using a fraction of the network's depth. At Samsung Research, the same tension appeared across language and vision-language models: personalizing a 1 GB language model on a mobile device overflowed 3 GB of memory, escalating complex queries to a server wasted prefill computation the device had already performed, and multi-turn vision-language reinforcement learning stalled on redundant visual preprocessing. Across these modalities, my work has focused on a single goal: designing representations, adaptation algorithms, and generation methods around both the information a task requires and the constraints of real-world deployment. Through the Master of Science in Intelligent Information Systems (MIIS) at Carnegie Mellon University’s Language Technologies Institute (LTI), I hope to deepen my foundation in language technologies and build intelligent information systems that are as practical to deploy as they are capable.
My work on task-aware representations began at IIIT Hyderabad, one of India’s most selective computer science institutes, which I entered in 2021 with a 99.95th-percentile score in JEE Main. In the Speech Processing Lab under Prof. Anil Kumar Vuppala, I first studied whispered-to-normal speech conversion, where a model must infer missing vocal-fold excitation from degraded spectral cues. Evaluating self-supervised acoustic targets from HuBERT improved reconstruction under whispered and noisy conditions, while spectral smoothing and principal-component selection controlled the synthesis cost of higher-dimensional features. That project led me to ask whether deep speech encoders carry similar redundancy across their layers—a question I tested in my undergraduate Honours research on distinguishing typical conversational disfluencies from atypical stuttering. Through a systematic layer-wise probing study across self-supervised speech transformers, I found that HuBERT’s fifth layer—only 5 of 24—achieved 97% F1 across disfluency categories, outperforming the final-layer representation while bypassing nearly 80% of the forward pass. Alongside contributing to the IIITH-TISA stuttered-speech corpus, I co-authored this work at Interspeech 2025, a flagship speech-processing conference. Discovering that task-discriminative structure often peaks in early layers sparked my interest in intermediate representations and early-exit architectures across speech and language models.
Alongside my thesis research, I learned what it takes to turn laboratory research into usable language-technology systems. To replace our group’s license-bound MATLAB workflows, I led six undergraduates in developing Waveform-Wizard, an open-source Python platform for interactive speech analysis that we presented at ICASSP 2025 Show & Tell with me as first student author. I then spent my summer internship at Samsung Research working on SnapLite, an on-device runtime serving over 200 production speech, vision, and language models. By unifying model deployment and validation across CPU, GPU, and NPU backends and introducing concurrent subgraph partitioning across two processors, I reduced time-to-first-inference tenfold—work adopted by multiple product teams after I presented it at Samsung Research’s annual conference. Building both research tools and production runtimes showed me that making language technologies usable requires co-designing model architectures with the systems that execute them.
When I returned to Samsung Research full-time to work with Samsung Research Korea on NNTrainer under an Advanced Research Fund project, the challenge shifted from static deployment to continual, privacy-preserving LLM personalization. Adapting a language model directly on a user’s device keeps personal data private, but conventional fine-tuning of a 1 GB model peaked at 3 GB of activation memory and ran too slowly on a mobile CPU. I first paired 4-bit quantized base weights with full-precision LoRA adapters trained under quantization-aware training—so the adapters compensated for quantization error—and combined weight checkpointing with selective activation recomputation to bring peak memory below 0.5 GB. To overcome slow CPU execution while per-layer synchronization left the NPU underutilized, I engineered an asynchronous CPU–NPU dispatch queue with zero-copy shared memory that accelerated training eightfold and resulted in a patent filing.
Even with asynchronous scheduling, however, backpropagation required maintaining backward graphs on NPU hardware built strictly for forward inference—a limitation that became acute on GGML’s Llama runtime, which offered a fast precompiled NPU forward path without backward-pass support. To redesign the adaptation algorithm around forward-only execution, I reformulated continual personalization using zeroth-order (ZO) optimization, estimating updates purely from forward evaluations. Because full-parameter perturbations exceeded device memory and dispatch limits, I restricted updates to a gradient-informed low-rank weight subspace and applied orthogonal projections across sequential tasks to prevent catastrophic forgetting. On a mobile NPU, this subspace ZO method matched or exceeded standard first-order LoRA in task accuracy and knowledge retention while completing 1,000 steps in 20 minutes—five times faster than prior on-device ZO training—and is currently under review at MLSys 2027. I am now extending this into a hybrid optimizer where the device runs local ZO updates on private data while a server periodically injects first-order guidance every K steps to reorient the low-rank subspace, studying how gradient fidelity and synchronization frequency govern continual language adaptation.
My interest in language-model algorithms has also extended from adaptation to collaborative generation. When a complex user query exceeds a lightweight on-device model’s capacity, standard routing restarts generation from the full context on the server, discarding the device’s work and repeating KV-cache prefill. Working with Dr. Prateek Kesarwani, I adapted speculative decoding into a computation-aware device–server handoff: because the on-device model has already encoded the context, it drafts candidate tokens that the server verifies in a single parallel pass, reusing the draft and KV state while using server-verified tokens to continually refine the local drafter online. In prototype evaluations against full-context server recomputation, this reduced end-to-end latency by 1.4 times and server computation by 2.8 times. Building directly on my Interspeech findings on intermediate representations, I am now evaluating intermediate-layer early exit for the draft model alongside on-device diffusion language models that draft token blocks in parallel while the server preserves long-context coherence.
More recently, I have brought this focus on generation and rollout efficiency to multimodal models, profiling multi-turn vision-language reinforcement learning rollouts for DeepEyes using Qwen2.5-VL and vLLM. In multi-turn visual reasoning, repeated image cropping and rollout-side CPU preprocessing bottlenecked GPU token generation across turns. By caching multimodal preprocessing and optimizing rollout-side execution, I reduced rollout-worker time by 20–30% on tested image sets while preserving generated token streams in paired comparisons. I am now investigating visual crop reuse and speculative decoding for RL rollout generation, exploring how decoding and caching algorithms can accelerate language generation conditioned on dynamic visual inputs without altering policy behavior.
Carnegie Mellon’s MIIS program is uniquely suited to help me unify this experience across speech, language, and multimodal systems. Through coursework such as Advanced Natural Language Processing (11-711) and Multimodal Machine Learning (11-777), I want to deepen my theoretical grounding in how linguistic, acoustic, and visual representations are learned and aligned—building on the mathematical maturity I developed as an undergraduate Teaching Assistant for Real Analysis and Linear Algebra. In parallel, Inference Algorithms for Language Modeling (11-664) and On-Device Machine Learning (11-767) directly complement my work on speculative decoding, early-exit generation, and resource-constrained LLM adaptation.

What draws me most to MIIS is the opportunity to pair this coursework with two semesters of faculty-supervised Directed Study and the collaborative Capstone project. I am particularly excited by Prof. Emma Strubell’s research in SLAB on computationally efficient NLP—ranging from hardware-aware adaptation to efficient reinforcement learning for reasoning (Act Only When It Pays)—which connects directly to both my low-rank subspace adaptation work and my multi-turn VLM RL rollout optimizations. Prof. Lei Li’s research on speculative LLM inference and parallel generation with layer-wise predictions aligns closely with my work on intermediate-layer early exit and diffusion drafters for collaborative decoding. I am equally drawn to Prof. Shinji Watanabe’s research in WAVLab on self-supervised speech representations and open speech foundation models, which builds naturally on my HuBERT disfluency research and open-source speech tooling. Through MIIS’s Directed Study and Capstone, I aim to grow into a research engineer who designs speech, language, and multimodal systems that bridge foundational modeling with efficient real-world deployment.
