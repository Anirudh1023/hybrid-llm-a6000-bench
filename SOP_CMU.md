A pretrained model’s default computation is not always the computation a task needs. I first encountered this in my undergraduate speech research at IIIT Hyderabad, where an intermediate layer of HuBERT outperformed its final layer at detecting speech disfluencies. At Samsung Research, similar questions arose while adapting and deploying language models: how could a model learn on a memory-constrained device, and how could device and server models coordinate generation without repeating work? These experiences shaped my interest in designing representations, adaptation algorithms, and generation methods around the needs of a task and its deployment environment. Through Carnegie Mellon’s Master of Science in Intelligent Information Systems, I hope to deepen my foundation in language technologies and learn to build capable, practical speech and language systems.

In IIIT Hyderabad’s Speech Processing Lab under Prof. Anil Kumar Vuppala, I worked on whispered-to-normal speech conversion. Because whispered speech lacks the vocal-fold excitation present in normal speech, the model must infer missing information from the remaining spectral cues. I explored richer spectral representations within HuBERT’s self-supervised pretraining, improving reconstruction under whispered and noisy conditions; spectral smoothing and principal-component selection helped control the cost of the higher-dimensional features. This work led me to ask what information is captured at different depths of a speech encoder and which representations a downstream task actually needs.

I pursued that question in my undergraduate Honours research on distinguishing typical disfluencies from atypical stuttering. Comparing layer-wise representations from self-supervised speech encoders, I found that HuBERT’s fifth layer, out of 24, achieved 97% F1 across disfluency categories and outperformed the final-layer representation. I also contributed to the IIITH-TISA stuttered-speech corpus and co-authored the resulting paper, published at Interspeech 2025. The result showed me that a model’s final representation is not necessarily its most informative one for every task. It sparked my interest in how intermediate representations could guide the design of task-specific speech and language architectures.

Alongside research, I led six undergraduates in developing Waveform-Wizard, an open-source Python platform that replaced our lab’s proprietary MATLAB speech-analysis workflows and was presented at ICASSP 2025. I then joined Samsung Research as an intern working on SnapLite, an on-device runtime serving over 200 production models. I built a unified deployment and verification pipeline across CPU, GPU, and NPU backends and added graph partitioning for concurrent execution across two processors. The pipeline reduced time to first inference tenfold and was adopted by multiple product teams. Building research tools and production runtimes made me interested in how model design and execution systems can be developed together.

After returning to Samsung Research full-time, I worked with Samsung Research Korea on NNTrainer through a project on privacy-preserving on-device LLM personalization. Fine-tuning a 1 GB model ran out of device memory. I combined 4-bit base weights with LoRA adapters trained using quantization-aware training, then introduced weight checkpointing and selective activation recomputation, reducing peak memory to under 0.5 GB. To address slow CPU execution, I designed an asynchronous CPU–NPU dispatch queue with zero-copy shared memory that overlapped data movement with computation. Training became eight times faster than CPU execution, and the execution design has been filed for a patent.

The project led me to ask whether the adaptation algorithm itself could be redesigned around the hardware. NNTrainer supported NPU backpropagation, while GGML’s Llama runtime offered a faster precompiled forward path but lacked backward support. I investigated zeroth-order optimization, which estimates updates using forward passes alone. Full-weight perturbations exceeded device memory and dispatch limits, so I restricted updates to a gradient-informed low-rank subspace and used orthogonal projections to retain knowledge across tasks. On a mobile NPU, the method matched or exceeded standard LoRA in accuracy and retention and completed 1,000 steps in 20 minutes—five times faster than prior on-device zeroth-order training. This work is under review at MLSys 2027. I am now investigating a hybrid optimizer in which the device performs local zeroth-order updates while a server periodically provides first-order guidance. I want to understand how the frequency and fidelity of that guidance affect convergence and continual adaptation.

My interest in language-model algorithms has also extended from adaptation to collaborative generation. Working with Dr. Prateek Kesarwani, I adapted speculative decoding for a device–server handoff: the device drafts candidate tokens while the server verifies them in parallel. Because the device generates the draft itself and retains its execution state, returning control to it can avoid re-prefilling a server-generated continuation. In prototype evaluations against full-context server recomputation, this reduced end-to-end latency by 1.4 times and server computation by 2.8 times. I am now exploring intermediate-layer early exits for the drafter and diffusion models that generate token blocks in parallel. These directions connect my interest in intermediate representations to a new question: how can models with different capabilities coordinate generation without repeating computation?

More recently, I have been working on multi-turn vision-language model reinforcement-learning rollouts for DeepEyes using Qwen2.5-VL and vLLM. By caching multimodal preprocessing and optimizing rollout-side CPU processing, I reduced rollout-worker time by 20–30% on tested image sets while preserving generated token streams in paired comparisons. I am continuing to investigate crop reuse and speculative decoding for rollout generation. This work has made me interested in how decoding methods can be adapted to language generation conditioned on visual inputs while preserving the model’s behavior.

I am also interested in Multimodal Machine Learning to understand how visual and linguistic representations are learned and aligned.
