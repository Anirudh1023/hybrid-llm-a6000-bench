The representations produced by a pretrained model are not equally useful for every task. I encountered this in my research on stuttered speech, where an intermediate layer of HuBERT outperformed its final layer at identifying disfluencies. Later, while working on language-model personalization at Samsung Research, I became interested in a related question: how should a model’s learning and generation methods be adapted to the setting in which it will be used? These experiences shaped my interest in speech and language models—from the representations they learn to the algorithms used to adapt them and generate responses. Through Carnegie Mellon’s Master of Science in Intelligent Information Systems, I hope to deepen my understanding of these models and learn to design them around the requirements of a task.

My interest in model representations began at IIIT Hyderabad’s Speech Processing Lab under Prof. Anil Kumar Vuppala. I worked on whispered-to-normal speech conversion, where a model must infer missing vocal-fold excitation from the spectral cues that remain in whispered speech. I explored richer spectral representations within HuBERT’s self-supervised pretraining to better capture these cues, improving reconstruction under whispered and noisy conditions. Because their higher dimensionality increased computational cost, I applied spectral smoothing and principal-component selection. The project introduced me to a question I want to study further: how can a model retain information that is useful for a language task while avoiding representations that add cost without improving performance?

I pursued the question of task-relevant representations in my undergraduate Honours research on distinguishing typical disfluencies from atypical stuttering. Comparing layer-wise representations from self-supervised speech encoders, I found that HuBERT’s fifth layer, out of 24, achieved 97% F1 across disfluency categories and outperformed the final-layer representation. I also contributed to the IIITH-TISA stuttered-speech corpus and co-authored the resulting paper, published at Interspeech 2025. The result showed me that a model’s final representation is not necessarily its most informative one for every task. It made me interested in how intermediate representations could guide the design of task-specific speech and language architectures.

To understand how models become usable on different hardware, I joined Samsung Research as an intern working on SnapLite, an on-device runtime serving over 200 production models. Separate vendor runtimes required backend-specific conversion and validation for CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs. I built a unified deployment and verification pipeline that preserved processor-specific kernel optimizations, and added graph partitioning for concurrent execution across two processors. The pipeline reduced time to first inference tenfold and was adopted by multiple product teams. This experience made me want to understand not only how models are executed, but also how learning algorithms could be designed with their eventual execution in mind.

After returning to Samsung Research full-time, I worked with Samsung Research Korea on NNTrainer, an open-source generative-AI engine, through a project on privacy-preserving on-device LLM personalization. Conventional fine-tuning of a 1 GB model peaked at 3 GB of memory, making adaptation impractical on the target device. I combined 4-bit base weights with LoRA adapters trained using quantization-aware training, and introduced weight checkpointing and selective activation recomputation. These changes reduced peak memory to under 0.5 GB. To address the remaining execution bottleneck, I designed an asynchronous CPU–NPU dispatch mechanism that overlapped data movement with computation, making training eight times faster than CPU execution. The execution design has been filed for a patent.

This work led me to ask whether the adaptation algorithm itself could be redesigned around the available hardware. GGML’s Llama runtime offered a faster precompiled forward path on the NPU but lacked backward support. I investigated zeroth-order optimization, which estimates updates using forward passes alone. Full-weight perturbations exceeded device memory and dispatch limits, so I restricted updates to a gradient-informed low-rank subspace and used orthogonal projections to retain knowledge across tasks. On a mobile NPU, the method matched or exceeded standard LoRA in accuracy and retention and completed 1,000 steps in 20 minutes—five times faster than prior on-device zeroth-order training. This work is under review at MLSys 2027. I am now investigating a hybrid optimizer in which the device performs local zeroth-order updates while a server periodically provides first-order guidance. Through this work, I want to better understand how the information available to an optimizer affects adaptation and retention, and how approximations can be evaluated across tasks.

My interest in language-model algorithms has also extended from adaptation to generation. I adapted speculative decoding for device–server generation: the device drafts candidate tokens while the server verifies them in parallel. In early prototype evaluations against a full-context server-recomputation baseline, this reduced end-to-end latency by 1.4 times and server computation by 2.8 times. The project made me interested in how models with different capabilities can cooperate during generation, and how decoding choices affect both the resulting text and the computation required to produce it.

More recently, I have been working on multi-turn vision-language model rollouts for DeepEyes using Qwen2.5-VL and vLLM. By caching multimodal preprocessing and optimizing rollout-side CPU processing, I reduced rollout-worker time by 20–30% on tested image sets while preserving generated token streams in paired comparisons. I am now investigating crop reuse and speculative decoding to reduce repeated visual processing and accelerate response generation. This work has made me interested in how decoding methods can be adapted to language generation conditioned on visual inputs, while preserving the behavior of the original model.

At Carnegie Mellon, I want to build the language-technology foundation needed to pursue these questions beyond the particular models and hardware I have worked with. Advanced Natural Language Processing would broaden my understanding of language tasks and modeling, helping me evaluate architectural choices in the context of what those tasks require. Inference Algorithms for Language Modeling would help me study how decoding strategies influence generation quality and computation, building on my speculative-decoding work. I am also interested in Multimodal Machine Learning to understand how visual and linguistic representations are aligned and combined during learning.

I am drawn to Prof. Emma Strubell’s research on the learning procedures and architectural choices needed for effective language models under computational constraints. Her questions connect to my work on layer-wise representations and low-rank adaptation, and I hope to learn how model and training choices can be evaluated across language tasks. Prof. Lei Li’s work on non-autoregressive translation with layer-wise predictions connects to my interest in intermediate representations and generation; it offers a way to think about how architectural choices can support parallel decoding while preserving task performance. I am also interested in Prof. Shinji Watanabe’s research on learned speech representations and end-to-end speech processing, which connects to my HuBERT work and would help me study how representations transfer across speech tasks.

Alongside research, I led six undergraduates in developing Waveform-Wizard, an open-source Python platform that replaced our lab’s proprietary MATLAB speech-analysis workflows and was presented at ICASSP 2025. I also served as an undergraduate teaching assistant for Real Analysis and Linear Algebra. I hope to bring this collaborative experience to MIIS while learning from peers who approach language technologies from different perspectives.

Through MIIS’s faculty-supervised directed study and collaborative capstone, I hope to apply these foundations to a language or multimodal system: identifying the information a task requires, evaluating representations and learning methods, and understanding the computational choices needed to put them into practice.
