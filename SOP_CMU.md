# Statement of Purpose

A task rarely needs every layer of a pretrained model, every parameter to be updated, or every computation in its default execution path. I first encountered this in speech research at IIIT Hyderabad, where choosing acoustic and intermediate representations changed what a model could reconstruct and detect. At Samsung Research, the same question arose in LLM personalization and generation: how much of a model needed to be adapted, which optimization methods could work within available hardware, and how could device and server models cooperate without repeating computation? These experiences shaped my interest in task-driven design of language and speech models: understanding how task requirements should shape model representations and architectures, learning and generation methods, and the computation used to train and deploy them. Through Carnegie Mellon’s Master of Science in Intelligent Information Systems, I hope to deepen my foundations in language technologies and investigate these questions across NLP, speech, and multimodal learning.

In IIIT Hyderabad’s Speech Processing Lab under Prof. Anil Kumar Vuppala, I worked on whispered-to-normal speech conversion. Since whispered speech lacks vocal-fold excitation, reconstructing normal speech requires inferring information from the remaining acoustic cues. I explored richer spectral representations in a HuBERT-based pipeline, improving reconstruction under whispered and noisy conditions. Their higher dimensionality increased computational cost, which I controlled through spectral smoothing and principal-component selection. This work introduced me to the relationship between acoustic representation, downstream performance, and the cost of processing different features.

I continued studying this relationship in my undergraduate Honours research on distinguishing typical disfluencies from atypical stuttering. Rather than relying on the final representation of a self-supervised speech encoder, I compared representations across its layers. HuBERT’s fifth layer, out of 24, achieved 97% F1 across disfluency categories and outperformed its final-layer representation. I also contributed to the IIITH-TISA stuttered-speech corpus and co-authored the resulting paper, published at Interspeech 2025. This result strengthened my interest in task-specific representation learning: a deeper model does not necessarily produce the most useful representation for every downstream task.

Alongside research, I led six undergraduates in developing Waveform-Wizard, an open-source Python platform that replaced our lab’s proprietary MATLAB speech-analysis workflows and was presented at ICASSP 2025 Show & Tell, with me as first student author. I then joined Samsung Research as an intern on SnapLite, an on-device inference runtime serving over 200 production models. I built a unified deployment and verification pipeline across CPU, GPU, and NPU backends, preserving processor-specific kernel optimizations, and added graph partitioning for concurrent execution across two processors. Adopted by multiple product teams, the pipeline reduced time to first inference tenfold. This experience gave me a foundation in model execution, compilation, and heterogeneous scheduling that I would later apply to language-model training and inference.

After returning to Samsung Research full-time, I worked with Samsung Research Korea on NNTrainer through a project on privacy-preserving on-device LLM personalization. The objective was to fine-tune a language model on user data without transferring that data off the device. Conventional fine-tuning of a 1 GB model peaked at 3 GB of memory. I combined 4-bit base weights with LoRA adapters trained using quantization-aware training, allowing the adapters to account for quantization error. Weight checkpointing and selective activation recomputation further reduced peak memory to under 0.5 GB. Once training fit within memory, CPU throughput became the bottleneck. I designed an asynchronous CPU–NPU dispatch queue with zero-copy shared memory to overlap data movement with computation, making training eight times faster than CPU-only execution. The execution design has been filed for a patent. This project gave me experience with parameter-efficient adaptation, quantization-aware training, and heterogeneous execution, while making me interested in how these choices jointly determine the capabilities and practical limits of personalized language models.

I then questioned whether adapting these models necessarily required backpropagation. NNTrainer supported NPU backpropagation, while GGML’s Llama runtime offered a faster precompiled forward path but lacked backward-pass support. I investigated zeroth-order optimization (ZO), which estimates parameter updates using forward evaluations alone. Full-weight perturbations exceeded device-memory and dispatch limits, so I restricted updates to a gradient-informed low-rank subspace and used orthogonal projections to retain knowledge across tasks. On a mobile NPU, the method matched or exceeded standard LoRA in accuracy and retention, completing 1,000 steps in 20 minutes—five times faster than prior on-device ZO training. This work, under review at MLSys 2027, showed me that model adaptation is not tied to a single optimization procedure: the learning method itself can be chosen around the model, task, and available computation. I am now investigating a hybrid optimizer in which the device performs local ZO updates while a server periodically supplies first-order guidance to reorient its low-dimensional search space. Beyond mobile personalization, I want to understand when hybrid first- and zeroth-order methods can make adaptation of larger language models more practical. In particular, I am interested in how to balance the memory and backward-computation savings of forward-only updates against their additional forward evaluations, and how the frequency and fidelity of server guidance affect convergence, communication, and continual adaptation.

My interest in language-model algorithms has also extended from adaptation to generation. Working with Dr. Prateek Kesarwani, I adapted speculative decoding to a device–server handoff in which the device drafts candidate tokens and a more capable server verifies them in parallel. By reusing the device's existing context and execution state, the handoff avoids recomputing the full context on the server. Against full-context server recomputation, this reduced end-to-end latency by 1.4 times and server computation by 2.8 times. I am now exploring intermediate-layer exits for the drafter and diffusion-based language models that generate token blocks in parallel. These investigations have made me interested in how generation algorithms and model architectures can be redesigned to exploit complementary model capabilities rather than relying exclusively on conventional autoregressive decoding.

More recently, I joined a collaborative effort on reinforcement learning for active video understanding. Rather than giving a vision-language model a fixed set of sampled frames, we are investigating how it can learn when to inspect additional temporal segments or spatial regions under a visual-token budget, and what evidence it should retain under a memory constraint. This extends my interest in task-specific learning and representation design to multimodal reasoning: teaching a model not only to interpret available information, but also to decide what information it needs to acquire.

MIIS appeals to me because its focus on extracting meaning from text, spoken language, and video brings together the areas in which I want to develop further. I would like to build a course of study around Advanced Natural Language Processing, Speech Technology for Conversational AI, Multimodal Machine Learning, and Inference Algorithms for Language Modeling. Advanced NLP would deepen my understanding of neural modeling and learning algorithms; Speech Technology for Conversational AI would let me study current speech foundation models and their applications; and Multimodal Machine Learning would strengthen my understanding of representation learning, alignment, and reasoning across modalities. Inference Algorithms for Language Modeling would let me examine how alternative generation strategies affect quality, controllability, and computational cost. Beyond coursework, MIIS's two-semester faculty-directed study, summer internship, and collaborative capstone would allow me to develop a research question and carry it through to a complete language or multimodal system.

I am particularly interested in Prof. Graham Neubig's research on core NLP algorithms, learning techniques, and language models associated with speech and other modalities. His work on learning from naturally occurring data also connects to my interest in developing more effective training and adaptation methods. Prof. Shinji Watanabe's research on self-supervised speech learning and multilingual speech foundation models would let me extend my HuBERT experience toward broader questions in speech representation and model adaptation. I am also drawn to Prof. Akari Asai's work on retrieval and inference-time algorithms for language models and tool-using agents. Her research connects to my emerging interest in how models can decide when additional evidence is necessary and how they should acquire it before generating an answer.

As an undergraduate teaching assistant for Real Analysis and Linear Algebra, I valued helping peers develop confidence with difficult ideas. I hope to bring the same collaborative approach to MIIS's directed study and capstone. Within the programme, I want to develop a deeper understanding of how learning objectives, model representations, and generation methods can be designed around the requirements of language and speech tasks, while learning to evaluate those choices in complete systems.
