A pretrained model’s default computation is not always what a task needs or a deployment setting can support. I first encountered this in my undergraduate speech research at IIIT Hyderabad, where the choice of spectral and intermediate representations affected how well a model reconstructed speech and detected disfluencies. At Samsung Research, related questions arose while adapting language models: how could an LLM learn from private data on a mobile device, and how could device and server models coordinate generation without repeating work? These experiences shaped my interest in designing speech and language models around the information a task requires, the learning methods available, and the setting in which the model will be used. Through Carnegie Mellon’s Master of Science in Intelligent Information Systems, I hope to deepen my understanding of language technologies and learn to develop models that are both effective for their tasks and practical to deploy.

In IIIT Hyderabad’s Speech Processing Lab under Prof. Anil Kumar Vuppala, I worked on whispered-to-normal speech conversion. Whispered speech lacks the vocal-fold excitation present in normal speech, so the model must infer missing information from the remaining spectral cues. I explored richer spectral representations within HuBERT’s self-supervised pretraining, improving reconstruction under whispered and noisy conditions. Their higher dimensionality increased computational cost, which I addressed through spectral smoothing and principal-component selection. The project showed me that improving a speech model begins with understanding which information is useful for the task, while also considering the cost of representing it.

I continued studying speech representations in my undergraduate Honours research on distinguishing typical disfluencies from atypical stuttering. Instead of relying only on the final representation of a self-supervised speech encoder, I compared representations across its layers. HuBERT’s fifth layer, out of 24, achieved 97% F1 across disfluency categories and outperformed the final-layer representation. I also contributed to the IIITH-TISA stuttered-speech corpus and co-authored the resulting paper, published at Interspeech 2025. Together, these projects made me interested in how speech models capture task-relevant information—from the features used during learning to the representations produced at different depths—and how that understanding can guide the design of effective models.

Alongside research, I led six undergraduates in developing Waveform-Wizard, an open-source Python platform that replaced our lab’s proprietary MATLAB speech-analysis workflows and was presented at ICASSP 2025. I then joined Samsung Research as an intern working on SnapLite, an on-device runtime serving over 200 production models. Separate vendor runtimes required backend-specific conversion and validation for CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs. I built a unified deployment and verification pipeline that preserved processor-specific kernel optimizations, and added graph partitioning for concurrent execution across two processors. The pipeline reduced time to first inference tenfold and was adopted by multiple product teams. Building research tools and production runtimes showed me how decisions beyond the model itself can determine whether it is practical to use.

After returning to Samsung Research full-time, I worked with Samsung Research Korea on NNTrainer through a project on privacy-preserving on-device LLM personalization. The goal was to adapt a language model using data that remained on the user’s device, but conventional fine-tuning of a 1 GB model ran out of memory. I combined 4-bit base weights with LoRA adapters trained using quantization-aware training, allowing the adapters to compensate for quantization error. I also introduced weight checkpointing and selective activation recomputation, reducing peak memory to under 0.5 GB. Once adaptation fit in memory, CPU throughput became the bottleneck. I designed an asynchronous CPU–NPU dispatch queue with zero-copy shared memory to overlap data movement with computation. Training became eight times faster than CPU execution, and the execution design has been filed for a patent.

This work led me to ask whether the adaptation algorithm itself could be redesigned for the hardware. NNTrainer supported NPU backpropagation, while GGML’s Llama runtime offered a faster precompiled forward path but lacked backward support. I investigated zeroth-order optimization, which estimates updates using forward passes alone. Because full-weight perturbations exceeded device memory and dispatch limits, I restricted updates to a gradient-informed low-rank subspace and used orthogonal projections to retain knowledge across tasks. On a mobile NPU, the method matched or exceeded standard LoRA in accuracy and retention and completed 1,000 steps in 20 minutes—five times faster than prior on-device zeroth-order training. This work is under review at MLSys 2027. I am now investigating a hybrid optimizer in which the device performs local zeroth-order updates while a server periodically provides first-order guidance. Across these projects, I have become interested in how the choice of update space and learning signal affects a language model’s ability to adapt and retain knowledge, and how those choices can be designed for the resources available.

My interest in language-model algorithms has also extended from adaptation to generation. Working with Dr. Prateek Kesarwani, I adapted speculative decoding for a device–server handoff: the device drafts candidate tokens while the server verifies them in parallel. Because the device generates the draft itself and retains its execution state, returning control to it can avoid re-prefilling a server-generated continuation. In prototype evaluations against full-context server recomputation, this reduced end-to-end latency by 1.4 times and server computation by 2.8 times. Building on my interest in intermediate representations, I am now exploring early exits for the drafter and diffusion models that generate token blocks in parallel.

More recently, I have been working on multi-turn vision-language model reinforcement-learning rollouts for DeepEyes using Qwen2.5-VL and vLLM. By caching multimodal preprocessing and optimizing rollout-side CPU processing, I reduced rollout-worker time by 20–30% on tested image sets while preserving generated token streams in paired comparisons. I am continuing to investigate crop reuse and speculative decoding for rollout generation. Together, these projects have made me want to study how language models with different capabilities can cooperate during generation, and how decoding methods can be adapted to visual inputs while preserving model behavior and reducing redundant computation.
