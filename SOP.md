Statement of Purpose
Anirudh Bocha · M.S. in Computer Science, UC San Diego
At Samsung Research, I worked on a practical obstacle to personalizing language models on mobile devices: fine-tuning a 1 GB model peaked at 3 GB of memory, while CPU execution was too slow for practical adaptation. Addressing these constraints required changes across both the learning algorithm and the execution system, from how model state was stored to how work was scheduled between the CPU and NPU. This experience shaped my interest in efficient machine learning: determining which representations and computations a task truly needs, and co-designing learning algorithms and runtime systems around those limits. Through the M.S. in Computer Science at UC San Diego, I hope to strengthen my foundations in machine learning and computer systems and extend this work to multimodal models and distributed server-scale execution as a research engineer.
My interest in this question began at IIIT Hyderabad, one of India’s most selective computer science institutes, which I entered in 2021 with a 99.95th-percentile score in JEE Main. In the Speech Processing Lab under Prof. Anil Kumar Vuppala, I first worked on whispered-to-normal speech conversion. Because whispered speech lacks vocal-fold excitation, a model must infer missing structure from the remaining spectral cues. I evaluated richer self-supervised acoustic targets from HuBERT to improve reconstruction under whispered and noisy conditions, and controlled their higher computational cost through spectral smoothing and principal-component selection. Balancing reconstruction quality against feature dimensionality led me to ask a similar question about network depth: does a downstream task actually need every layer of a deep encoder?
I tested that question in my undergraduate Honours research on distinguishing typical speech disfluencies from atypical stuttering. Comparing layer-wise representations across self-supervised speech encoders, I found that HuBERT’s fifth layer—only 5 of 24—achieved 97% F1 across disfluency categories and outperformed the final-layer representation while bypassing nearly 80% of the network. I also helped build the IIITH-TISA stuttered-speech corpus and co-authored the resulting paper at Interspeech 2025, a flagship speech-processing conference. Because a single early layer sufficed where all 24 were normally run, I became interested in task- and input-adaptive depth—such as early-exit mechanisms and sparse activations—and in testing whether theoretical compute savings translate into real runtime speedups on hardware.
To see how model-level savings behave on real silicon, I joined Samsung Research as a summer intern working on SnapLite, an on-device inference runtime supporting over 200 production models. Deploying models across CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs required separate conversion, maintenance, and validation for each vendor runtime. I built a unified deployment and verification pipeline that preserved processor-specific kernel optimizations, and added graph partitioning to execute subgraphs concurrently across two processors. This cut time to first inference tenfold and was adopted by multiple product teams after I presented it at Samsung Research’s annual conference. Working on SnapLite showed me how graph compilation, placement, and cross-processor scheduling govern real-world latency alongside individual operator cost—though it optimized only models whose parameters were already fixed.
After returning to Samsung Research full-time, I collaborated with Samsung Research Korea on NNTrainer, Samsung’s open-source generative-AI engine, under an Advanced Research Fund project on privacy-preserving on-device LLM personalization. Fine-tuning a 1 GB model peaked at 3 GB of memory because backpropagation had to retain or recompute intermediate activations across layers. I combined 4-bit base weights with full-precision LoRA adapters trained under quantization-aware training so the adapters absorbed deployment-time quantization error, and added weight checkpointing, selective activation recomputation, and memory-mapped loading to reduce peak memory below 0.5 GB.
Once training fit in memory, CPU throughput became the bottleneck. Moving compute-heavy operations to the NPU initially left its matrix engines underutilized because layer-by-layer synchronization and CPU–NPU data transfers erased kernel-level gains. I therefore engineered an asynchronous dispatch queue with zero-copy shared memory to overlap data movement with computation, making training 8× faster than CPU execution and leading to a patent filing on the CPU–NPU execution design. Working through these successive bottlenecks showed me how tightly memory lifecycles and asynchronous scheduling are coupled, while also exposing a deeper limit: every systems fix so far changed how training ran, not what backpropagation computed.
That realization led me to investigate whether the adaptation algorithm itself could be redesigned around forward-only hardware. While NNTrainer supported NPU backpropagation, GGML’s Llama runtime offered a faster precompiled forward path on the NPU without backward-pass support. To enable adaptation on that runtime, I reformulated fine-tuning around zeroth-order (ZO) optimization, which estimates updates from forward passes alone. Because full-weight perturbations pushed 3.2 GB through a single dispatch and overflowed device memory, I restricted updates to a gradient-informed low-rank subspace and used orthogonal projections to retain knowledge across sequential tasks. On a mobile NPU, this method matched or exceeded standard LoRA in accuracy and retention while completing 1,000 steps in 20 minutes—5× faster than prior on-device ZO training. This work, under review at MLSys 2027, the Conference on Machine Learning and Systems, demonstrated that hardware constraints can reshape the optimizer itself, though a single device remains bounded by its local compute budget.
To push past a single device’s compute ceiling without exporting private user data to a server, I am now developing a hybrid optimizer in which the device performs local ZO updates on private examples while a server contributes a first-order signal every K steps to reorient the device’s low-dimensional subspace. Because directional guidance rather than raw data crosses the boundary, the device spends fewer forward evaluations finding productive update directions. Measuring how much on-device computation this saves for a target personalization quality—and how large K can grow before stale guidance slows convergence—has motivated me to deepen my graduate training in distributed and low-rank optimization alongside systems design.
A parallel boundary problem arises in hybrid inference when complex requests exceed a lightweight on-device model. Standard escalation restarts generation on the server from the full context, discarding the device’s work and repeating KV-cache prefill that hybrid serving is meant to save. Working with Dr. Prateek Kesarwani, I adapted speculative decoding—built to accelerate a single autoregressive model—to this cross-tier handoff: because the device model already holds the context, it drafts candidate tokens that the server verifies in one parallel pass, a computation-aware handoff reuses the draft and KV state, and online learning from server-verified tokens continually improves the device as a drafter. Against full-context server recomputation, this reduced end-to-end latency by 1.4× and server computation by 2.8×. Following my Interspeech result on intermediate representations, I am now testing intermediate-layer early exit for the draft model alongside on-device diffusion language models, which decode faster locally while the server preserves long context.
More recently, I have begun studying redundant computation in vision-language model reinforcement learning. I profiled multi-turn rollouts for DeepEyes using Qwen2.5-VL and vLLM, where repeated image processing and rollout-side CPU execution delayed GPU generation across turns. Optimizing multimodal preprocessing and rollout-side CPU execution reduced rollout-worker time by 20–30% on tested image sets while preserving generated token streams in paired comparisons, and I am continuing to investigate crop reuse and speculative decoding for rollout generation. Moving from edge runtimes to vLLM rollout pipelines exposed how visual token volume, KV-cache growth, and CPU–GPU scheduling interact in parallel multimodal workloads—a setting I am eager to explore further during my master’s.
Alongside research, I led six undergraduates in developing Waveform-Wizard, an open-source Python platform that replaced our lab’s proprietary MATLAB speech-analysis workflows and was presented at ICASSP 2025 Show & Tell with me as first student author. I also served as an undergraduate Teaching Assistant for Real Analysis and Linear Algebra. These experiences showed me how much I value building open tools with others and helping peers develop confidence with rigorous mathematical ideas, which I hope to continue at UC San Diego through the IDEA Engineering Student Center.
Across speech encoders, on-device LLM runtimes, and multimodal rollout pipelines, I have approached efficiency one bottleneck at a time—compressing representations and depth, overlapping heterogeneous execution, projecting updates into low-rank subspaces, and coordinating work between device and server. Through an M.S. in Computer Science at UC San Diego, I want to combine advanced coursework in optimization and machine learning systems (CSE 291A) with thesis research (CSE 298) to study how these levers generalize across multimodal architectures and distributed serving systems. In Z Lab, Prof. Zhijian Liu’s work on algorithm–system co-design aligns directly with my trajectory: DFlash, which uses block diffusion for speculative drafting, connects directly to my work on diffusion drafters and speculative handoff, NVILA connects to reducing visual-token redundancy in multimodal pipelines, and ParoQuant relates closely to the 4-bit quantization constraints I tackled in NNTrainer. I am equally drawn to Prof. Hao Zhang’s research in Hao AI Lab on LLM serving and distributed systems, such as DistServe, where I hope to evaluate intermediate-layer early exit, KV-state reuse, and speculative execution at cluster scale, preparing me for a career as a research engineer building scalable, hardware-aware ML systems.
