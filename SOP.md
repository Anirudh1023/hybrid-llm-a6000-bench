At Samsung Research, I worked on a practical obstacle to personalizing language models on mobile devices: fine-tuning a 1 GB model exceeded the device’s available memory, while CPU execution was too slow. Addressing these constraints required changes across the learning algorithm and the execution system, from how model state was stored to how work was scheduled between the CPU and NPU. This experience shaped my interest in efficient machine learning: understanding which representations and computations a task needs, and designing learning algorithms and systems around those requirements. Through an M.S. in Computer Science, I hope to strengthen my foundations in machine learning and computer systems and extend this work to multimodal models and distributed execution.

My interest in this question began at IIIT Hyderabad’s Speech Processing Lab, where I worked with Prof. Anil Kumar Vuppala on whispered-to-normal speech conversion. Whispered speech lacks the vocal-fold excitation present in normal speech, so a model must infer missing information from the remaining spectral cues. I explored richer spectral representations within HuBERT’s self-supervised pretraining to improve reconstruction under whispered and noisy conditions. Their higher dimensionality, however, increased computational cost. By applying spectral smoothing and principal-component selection, I began to understand the relationship between the information a representation preserves and the resources needed to use it.

I pursued the question of task-relevant representations in my undergraduate Honours research on distinguishing typical disfluencies from atypical stuttering. Comparing layer-wise representations from self-supervised speech encoders, I found that HuBERT’s fifth layer, out of 24, achieved 97% F1 across disfluency categories and outperformed the final-layer representation. I also contributed to the IIITH-TISA stuttered-speech corpus and co-authored the resulting paper, published at Interspeech 2025. The result showed me that a model’s final representation is not necessarily its most informative one for every task. It made me interested in how intermediate representations could guide the design of task-specific models and, potentially, how much computation they require.

To understand how these model-level questions translate to hardware, I joined Samsung Research as an intern working on SnapLite, an on-device runtime serving over 200 production models. Deploying a model across CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs required separate conversion and validation for vendor-specific runtimes. I built a unified deployment and verification pipeline that preserved processor-specific kernel optimizations, and added graph partitioning for concurrent execution across two processors. The pipeline reduced time to first inference tenfold and was adopted by multiple product teams after I presented it at Samsung Research’s annual conference. The project showed me that compilation, placement, and scheduling can shape model performance alongside the cost of individual operations. I became interested in how these decisions could be made jointly with the algorithms they support.

After returning to Samsung Research full-time, I worked with Samsung Research Korea on NNTrainer, an open-source generative-AI engine, through a project on privacy-preserving on-device language model personalization. Fine-tuning a 1 GB model peaked at 3 GB of memory because the device had to hold model weights and retained activations. I combined 4-bit base weights with LoRA adapters trained using quantization-aware training, then introduced weight checkpointing and selective activation recomputation. Together, these changes reduced peak memory use to under 0.5 GB.

Once memory was no longer the primary constraint, CPU throughput became the bottleneck. I moved compute-intensive operations to the NPU, but synchronization and data transfers left its matrix engines underutilized. To address this, I designed an asynchronous dispatch queue with zero-copy shared memory, allowing data movement to overlap with computation. Training became eight times faster than CPU execution, and the CPU–NPU execution design has been filed for a patent. Working through these successive bottlenecks taught me to consider learning algorithms and execution systems together: an algorithmic improvement can expose a systems constraint, and vice versa.

That experience led me to investigate whether the adaptation algorithm itself could be redesigned around the hardware. NNTrainer supported NPU backpropagation, while GGML’s Llama runtime offered a faster precompiled forward path but lacked backward support. I explored zeroth-order optimization, which estimates updates using forward passes alone, to make adaptation possible on that runtime. Full-weight perturbations exceeded device memory and dispatch limits, so I restricted updates to a gradient-informed low-rank subspace and used orthogonal projections to retain knowledge across tasks. On a mobile NPU, the method matched or exceeded standard LoRA in accuracy and retention and completed 1,000 steps in 20 minutes—five times faster than prior on-device zeroth-order training. This work is under review at MLSys 2027.

The project made me interested in how approximate optimization methods can preserve adaptation quality while using less computation and state. I am now investigating a hybrid optimizer in which a device performs local zeroth-order updates while a server periodically provides first-order guidance to reorient the update subspace. In particular, I want to understand how frequently guidance is needed before stale directions affect convergence. Studying this question has shown me the value of a stronger theoretical foundation in optimization alongside practical experience with model training.

As I worked within the limits of a single device, I also became interested in how coordinating computation with a server could make inference more efficient. I adapted speculative decoding for device–server generation: the device drafts candidate tokens while the server verifies them in parallel. In early prototype evaluations against a full-context server-recomputation baseline, this reduced end-to-end latency by 1.4 times and server computation by 2.8 times. The project led me to think about how computation and execution state should be distributed across tiers, and how decoding algorithms can take advantage of models with different capabilities and resource budgets.

More recently, I have begun studying redundant computation in vision-language model reinforcement learning. I profiled multi-turn rollouts for DeepEyes using Qwen2.5-VL and vLLM, then improved multimodal preprocessing and rollout-side CPU execution. On tested image sets, these changes reduced rollout-worker time by 20–30% while preserving generated token streams in paired comparisons. I am continuing to investigate crop reuse and speculative decoding for rollout generation. This work has broadened my interest from individual device execution to the scheduling and memory-management challenges of parallel multimodal workloads.

My work has given me experience addressing efficiency problems in specific model and hardware settings. During my master’s, I want to strengthen the foundations needed to reason about them more broadly: optimization for understanding approximate learning methods, machine learning for evaluating how architectural choices affect model quality, and computer systems for managing computation and state across accelerators. Through advanced coursework and research, I hope to study how task-specific representations and learning algorithms can be combined with scheduling and resource management for efficient training and inference.

Alongside research, I led six undergraduates in developing Waveform-Wizard, an open-source Python platform that replaced our lab’s proprietary MATLAB speech-analysis workflows and was presented at ICASSP 2025. I also served as an undergraduate teaching assistant for Real Analysis and Linear Algebra. These experiences showed me how much I value building tools with others and helping peers develop confidence with difficult technical ideas.

My goal is to become a research engineer designing scalable, hardware-aware ML systems. Graduate study will help me build on my experience across speech, language models, and on-device execution while developing the theoretical and systems foundations to make capable models practical across a wider range of computational environments.
